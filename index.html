MODULE 00

What FEA actually does

Before clicking anything: what the solver is computing, and what it needs from you.

A real part is continuous: every point in it can move a little. That's infinitely many unknowns, which no computer can solve. Finite element analysis chops the part into a finite number of small chunks (elements) joined at points (nodes). The solver only figures out how much each node moves. Everything else (strain, stress) is calculated from those node movements.

AnalogyThink of the part as a 3D net of springs. Each element is a little block of springs whose stiffness comes from its shape and material (Young's modulus E, Poisson's ratio ν). Push on the net somewhere, pin it somewhere else, and the solver finds how every knot in the net moves so that all the spring forces balance.

The one equation

A linear static structural analysis solves one (very big) matrix equation:

[K] {u} = {F} [K] global stiffness matrix (from geometry + material + mesh) {u} displacement of every node (THE UNKNOWNS) {F} forces on every node (from your loads)

Notice what you supply: geometry and material (to build K), loads (to build F), and supports (which set some entries of u to known values, usually zero). That's the whole job. Every setting in Mechanical is one of those three things, or a knob on how accurately they get built.

Displacement is the answer, stress is derived

Once the solver has {u}, strain comes from how the displacement changes across each element (a derivative), and stress comes from strain through the material law (σ = Eε in 1D). Two consequences that matter for the rest of this site:

  • Displacements converge faster than stresses. A coarse mesh gives decent deflection but can badly miss peak stress, because stress is a derivative of an approximation.
  • Stresses are computed inside elements and then averaged to the nodes for those smooth contour plots. Where elements disagree a lot, the average hides error. (Module 13 covers this.)

Why supports are mandatory

If nothing holds the part, it can slide or spin freely without straining at all. Mathematically K is singular (no unique answer), and the solver errors out with something like "the model is underconstrained" or "rigid body motion". You need enough supports to stop all 6 rigid body motions in 3D: 3 translations (X, Y, Z) and 3 rotations (about X, Y, Z).

Linear vs nonlinear, in one paragraph

The equation above is linear: double the load, double every displacement and stress. It solves in one shot. It stays linear only if three things are true: (1) the material stays elastic (no yielding), (2) deflections are small enough that the geometry basically doesn't change, (3) contacts don't open, close, or slide. Break any of these and the solve becomes nonlinear: Mechanical applies the load in steps and iterates (Newton-Raphson) until each step balances. Slower, can fail to converge, but sometimes necessary. You'll see these flagged throughout with a nonlinear tag.

The workflow you'll repeat forever

  1. Define the question. What failure are you checking? Yield? Deflection? Stiffness? Fatigue at a weld? This decides everything below.
  2. Material (Engineering Data).
  3. Geometry, simplified to what matters for the question.
  4. Connections between bodies (contacts, joints).
  5. Mesh, fine where the answer lives.
  6. Supports and loads that represent reality honestly.
  7. Solve.
  8. Check: reactions = applied loads, deformation looks physical, mesh converged, hand calc agrees within reason.
  9. Then believe the stress number.
Garbage in, rainbow outMechanical will happily produce a beautiful red-to-blue contour plot from a wrong model. The color plot is never evidence on its own. Step 8 is not optional.

Linking systems (sharing data)

The real power of the schematic is dragging one system onto a cell of another to share data. A link line appears and the shared cells go gray in the second system. Common ones:

  • Static Structural → Eigenvalue Buckling. Drop Buckling onto the Static system's Solution cell. Buckling needs the prestressed state from the static run.
  • Modal → Harmonic / Random Vibration / Response Spectrum. Mode-superposition analyses reuse the modes.
  • Steady-State Thermal → Static Structural. Drop Static onto the Thermal Solution cell to bring temperatures in as a load (thermal stress).
  • Sharing geometry/model only. Drop a second system onto the first one's Model cell to run a modal and a static on the identical mesh without remeshing.

Parameters and Design Points

Any number with a little empty box next to it in Mechanical's Details pane (a dimension from CAD, a force magnitude, a result maximum) can be made a parameter by clicking the box (it turns into a blue P). A Parameter Set bar appears on the schematic. Open it and you get a table of Design Points: change a thickness from 3 to 4 to 5 mm in rows, hit Update All Design Points, and Workbench re-runs everything and fills in the max stress for each. This is how you do quick trade studies without clicking through the model ten times.

Files: the part that bites everyone once

A project is a .wbpj file plus a folder named projectname_files. The .wbpj is tiny. The folder holds everything. Copy one without the other and the project is dead. To send a project to someone, use File › Archive, which makes a single .wbpz. Open that with File › Restore Archive.

Lab computer warningResults files get big fast (hundreds of MB to GB). Before archiving, right-click the Solution cell → Clear Generated Data if you don't need results, or uncheck "results" in the archive options.

Units

Workbench has its own unit menu (Units on the project page) and Mechanical has another (Home › Units in Mechanical). They're independent. Mechanical converts everything internally, so you can enter a force in lbf and read stress in MPa. The usual student pick is Metric (mm, kg, N, s, mV, mA), which gives stress in MPa directly. Just look at the unit next to every value you type.

Adding a material from the library

  1. In Engineering Data, click Engineering Data Sources (the book icon at the top). The top pane now shows libraries: General Materials, Aerospace, etc.
  2. Click General Materials (or whichever). Find e.g. Aluminum Alloy.

Nonlinear materials (later)

If you need to know what happens after yield (permanent set, crush), add a plasticity model like Bilinear Isotropic Hardening (yield strength + tangent modulus). That makes the analysis nonlinear. For "is my part below yield with margin" questions, stay linear and compare to yield yourself.

MODULE 03

Geometry prep

The CAD model you'd send to a machinist is usually the wrong model to mesh.

Getting CAD in

Right-click the Geometry cell → Import Geometry › Browse. Use STEP (.step/.stp) or Parasolid (.x_t) from SolidWorks; both are clean. If SolidWorks is installed on the same machine you can also read .sldprt/.sldasm directly. Double-click Geometry to open it in SpaceClaim (or Discovery in newer versions; older courses use DesignModeler). Everything in this module can be done in any of them.

What to simplify (defeaturing)

Ask: does this feature change stiffness or stress where I care? If not, delete it. Small features force tiny elements, which balloon solve time and create fake stress hot spots.

Usually removeCosmetic fillets far from the region of interest, logos and engraved text, tiny chamfers, threads (model bolts as smooth cylinders), small holes in low-stress areas, sliver faces from bad CAD exports.
Usually keepFillets in the high-stress region (they control the peak stress!), holes near the load path, weld fillets if you're checking the weld toe, anything that changes stiffness meaningfully.

In SpaceClaim: the Repair tab finds split edges, small faces, and gaps. Pull with the Fill option deletes fillets/rounds (select the fillet face, press Delete also works on most features).

Solids, shells, and beams

A thin part (sheet metal, tube wall) meshed with solid elements needs several elements through the thickness to capture bending, which is expensive. Instead you can reduce dimensionality:

Body typeWhenHow in SpaceClaimMechanical needs
SolidChunky parts: uprights, brackets, hubsDefaultNothing extra
Surface (shell)Thickness < ~1/10 of other dimensions: sheet metal, thin-wall tubes, gussetsPrepare › MidsurfaceThickness assigned to the surface body
Line (beam)Long slender members: frame tubes, truss bars, tie rodsPrepare › Extract or draw lines; assign a cross-sectionCross Section (e.g. circular tube OD/ID)
Baja frame exampleA whole roll cage as solids is millions of elements. As line bodies with 1.25" × 0.065" tube sections it's a few thousand beam elements and solves in seconds. You get member forces and bending stresses, but not stress at the weld cluster itself. For that, model just the node of interest in solids or shells (submodeling).

Multibody parts and Shared Topology

If two bodies are touching, Mechanical by default creates a bonded contact between them (Module 06). An alternative is Shared Topology: put the bodies in one component and set Workbench › Share in SpaceClaim (or in DesignModeler: form a multibody part). Then the mesh nodes on the shared face are literally the same nodes, so there is no contact at all. It's cleaner and faster for welded assemblies, especially shell/beam models where bonded contact between edges is fiddly.

Symmetry: cut the model in half (or eighth)

If geometry, material, and loads are all symmetric about a plane, you can model half and apply a symmetry boundary on the cut. Half the elements, same answer. Split in SpaceClaim with Design › Split Body using a plane, delete one half, then in Mechanical apply a Frictionless Support or a Symmetry Region on the cut face (Module 09). Loads applied to the half model must be halved too if they straddle the plane.

Don't use symmetry for modal or bucklingunless you understand it. A half model only finds modes that are symmetric about the plane; it misses all the antisymmetric ones.

Named Selections from CAD

In SpaceClaim, select faces and create a Group (Groups panel, "Create NS"). Groups come into Mechanical as Named Selections. If you name the load faces and support faces in CAD, they survive geometry changes much better than faces picked by hand in Mechanical, which can lose their scoping when the geometry updates.

Scoping: telling Mechanical "where"

Almost every object (support, load, mesh control, result) needs a Scope: the geometry it applies to. The pattern is always the same:

  1. Set the selection filter in the toolbar: Body, Face, Edge, or Vertex (shortcuts Ctrl+B, Ctrl+F, Ctrl+E, Ctrl+P).
  2. Click the geometry. Ctrl+click to add more. Click-and-drag paints across multiple faces.
  3. In the Details pane, click the yellow Geometry field and press Apply.

Or switch Scoping Method to Named Selection and pick one from the dropdown. For anything you'll reuse (a load face, the area you'll refine the mesh at, the face you'll probe), make a Named Selection: select it, right-click → Create Named Selection.

Selecting hidden facesHover over a spot and the Select Through ladder pops up at bottom-left (stacked colored rectangles). Each rectangle is a face under the cursor, front to back. Click one to grab a face you can't see. Also: right-click a body → Hide Body to see inside an assembly.

Body-level settings worth knowing

Click a body under Geometry:

  • Stiffness Behavior: Flexible (default, meshed and deforms) or Rigid (no mesh, moves as a single block; great for big stiff parts you only need to transmit load through).
  • Suppressed: Yes removes the body from the analysis without deleting it from CAD. Right-click → Suppress Body.
  • Assignment: the material.
  • Thickness: only for surface bodies.

Coordinate systems

Everything defaults to the Global Coordinate System from CAD. If your load points along a weird angle (a steering link at 23°), make a local one: right-click Coordinate Systems → Insert › Coordinate System, scope it to a face or set origin and axes by hand. Any load or support that has a Coordinate System field can then use it. Cylindrical coordinate systems (Type: Cylindrical) are used for radial/tangential results and for loads like radial displacement.

MODULE 05

Coming from SolidWorks Simulation

If you already know SW Sim, this is the translation layer: same ideas, different names, and a few things Ansys just does better.

SolidWorks Simulation is a perfectly good place to learn: fixtures, external loads, mesh, run, look at von Mises. Everything transfers. Ansys is famously less friendly, but it meshes far denser (even the Student version), has many more support and load types, and doesn't need workarounds to stay stable under big forces. So the goal of this module is to get you doing in Ansys what you already do in SW Sim, then point at what's next.

Step 0: export a STEP, not an STL

In SolidWorks, File › Save As › STEP AP214 (*.step). An STL (what you send to a 3D printer) is just a shell of triangles, and a DXF (what you send to a laser cutter) is a 2D outline. A STEP stores the real, equation-based geometry: true cylinders, true fillets, and your split lines. Split lines come through as separate faces, which is exactly how you make load patches (Module 11).

The click path, start to finish

  1. Open Workbench. The app isn't called "Ansys" in your Start menu, it's Workbench 2025 R2 (or whatever year).
  2. Drag Static Structural from the Toolbox list on the left into the empty Project Schematic. That's your standard linear static FEA, the equivalent of a SW "Static" study.
  3. Right-click Geometry → Import Geometry → Browse and pick your STEP.
  4. Right-click Setup → Edit. Mechanical opens. This is where supports, mesh, loads, and results live.
  5. Material: right-click Materials → Insert › Material Assignment, select your body, Apply, then click the arrow next to Material Name and search for your material. (Add custom ones like S7 tool steel in Engineering Data first, Module 02.) Or just click the body under Geometry and set Assignment.
  6. Mesh: click Mesh, then Sizing in the ribbon. Select the body, Apply, set Element Size.
  7. Supports: click Static Structural in the tree, then pick from the Supports dropdown (or the Fixed / Frictionless / Displacement shortcuts beside it). Select faces with Ctrl+click to grab many at once, Apply.
  8. Loads: still on the Static Structural context tab, pick from Loads or Inertial.
  9. Results: click Solution; the ribbon shows Deformation, Strain, Stress, etc. Add Total Deformation and Equivalent (von-Mises) before solving (adding them after is fine too, it doesn't re-solve).
  10. Solve (lightning bolt). It can look like nothing is happening. Click the progress bar at the bottom left for status.

Details pane habits worth copying

  • Define By: Components instead of the default Vector. You type X, Y, Z directly, so there's no guessing which way the arrow points.
  • Applied By (on a Force on faces): Surface Effect (default) distributes the load through a layer of surface elements; Direct puts the nodal forces straight onto the face nodes. For most work they agree closely; pick one and be consistent.
  • Units next to every value. Rotational Velocity defaults to rad/s. 15,000 RPM = 1570.8 rad/s; 18,000 RPM = 1885 rad/s. Switch with Home › Units › Angular Velocity if you'd rather think in RPM.
  • Rotational Velocity axis: define by components (e.g. Z = ω) and set the X/Y/Z Coordinate to a point on the spin axis, usually the hub center or the part's center of mass. Wrong axis location = wobbling disk = huge fake stress.

Mesh limits you'll actually hit

The Student version caps the problem size (Module 07), and your CPU caps patience. As a reference point, a ~200 mm combat-robot weapon disk meshed fine at a 1 mm body sizing in Student. Lab-license Ansys can go orders of magnitude finer. The rule from the SW world still holds: at least 3–4 elements across the narrowest section you care about, and refine locally rather than everywhere.

Worked example: spinning weapon disk (centrifugal + impact)

PartFlat weapon disk with a tooth, lightening pockets, hub bore + bolt holes
MaterialS7 tool steel: E = 205 GPa, ν = 0.28, ρ = 7800 kg/m³, yield ≈ 1650 MPa
SupportHub bore + bolt holes
Load cases(a) spin at speed, (b) impact on the tooth

(a) Centrifugal: is it going to burst?

  1. Support: Fixed Support on the hub bore and all the bolt-hole faces (Ctrl+click them all, one support). Spinning is self-balanced radially, so this mostly just stops the disk floating.
  2. Load: Inertial › Rotational Velocity, scoped to All Bodies, Define By Components, Z = ω about the hub center. Use at least 1.5× the max RPM you plan to run.
  3. Mesh: body sizing small enough for 3–4 elements across the thinnest spoke; local face sizing on the pocket fillets.
  4. Results: Equivalent Stress and Total Deformation. Read the peak, typically at the pocket corners and spoke roots.
The ω² catchCentrifugal stress scales with ω², not ω. Running the sim at 1.5× RPM already multiplies stress by 2.25. So "FoS 2 to 2.5 at 1.5× max RPM" is really a factor of about 4.5 to 5.6 at the speed you'll actually run. That's not wrong for a combat weapon (fatigue, impact pre-stress, material scatter), just know what you're asking for. If you want FoS 2 at operating speed, solve at operating speed and compare directly.
Hand-check bounds (outer radius R = 100 mm, S7, ρ = 7.8e-9 t/mm³): ω at 18,000 RPM = 1885 rad/s Solid plain disk, peak at center: σ = (3+ν)/8 · ρ ω² R² = 0.41 × 277 ≈ 114 MPa Disk with a small bore, peak at bore: σ ≈ (3+ν)/4 · ρ ω² R² ≈ 227 MPa (a hole ~doubles it) Thin rim / ring: σ = ρ ω² R² ≈ 277 MPa Pockets and teeth push real peaks above the plain-disk value (Kt at corners), so expect FEA somewhere around and above these. Way off? Check axis & units.

(b) Impact: where will it crack?

  1. Support: same Fixed Support on the hub. (No inertia relief needed: that's for models with no supports at all. In SW Sim the impact case needed inertial relief + large displacement to stop it blowing up; Ansys handles the plain linear setup fine.)
  2. Load: Force on the tooth's striking face, Define By Components, pointing into the face (tangential to the disk rotation).
  3. Magnitude: a "big number" like 100,000 lbf (≈ 445 kN) is a fine way to light up the stress concentrations.
  4. Look for hot spots, not numbers: tooth root, spokes, thin sections near pockets. Fix with bigger fillets and thicker walls, re-run, compare.
Linear = you don't need to keep increasing the forceIn a linear static run, every stress scales exactly with the load. The hot-spot pattern is identical at 1 lbf and 100,000 lbf, so the magnitude only matters for absolute numbers. To find the critical load, solve once and scale:
F_critical = F_applied × (σ_yield / σ_peak)
e.g. 445 kN gives 2,900 MPa peak in S7 → F_crit ≈ 445 × 1650/2900 ≈ 253 kN. (Turning on Large Deflection breaks this scaling, which is one reason a linear run is the better tool for comparing geometries.)

The honest caveat: a real hit is a few milliseconds of contact. Peak force depends on impact speed, the weapon's stored energy (½Iω²), and how stiff both robots are, none of which a static run knows. Static FEA is excellent for comparing designs and finding weak spots, but for "will it survive a 250 km/h tooth hit" the right tool is explicit dynamics (below).

Why 2D works for a weapon disk

A flat disk loaded in its own plane (spin, a hit on the tooth face) has stress that barely varies through its thickness. Plane stress assumes exactly that: σz = 0, everything happens in the XY plane, and the thickness just scales stiffness and load capacity. A 2D mesh at 0.2 mm is often cheaper than a 3D mesh at 1 mm, so you can resolve fillet peaks you can't afford in 3D. It stops being valid if the part has thickness steps, out-of-plane loads (a hit that twists the disk), or features like countersinks that vary through thickness.

MODULE 06

Connections: contacts, joints, springs

How separate bodies talk to each other. Get this wrong and load either can't get through or gets through in the wrong way.

Separate bodies don't know about each other unless something connects them. When you open Mechanical, it auto-detects faces that are touching (within a tolerance) and creates Contact Regions under Connections › Contacts. All of them default to Bonded. That default is often fine and sometimes badly wrong.

The five contact types

TypeCan separate?Can slide?Linear?Use for
BondedNoNoYesWelds, glue, parts you're treating as one piece, quick first runs of bolted joints
No SeparationNoYes (μ=0)YesRarely; sliding interfaces where you know they stay in contact
FrictionlessYesYes (μ=0)NoPin in a hole, part resting on another, interference fits
RoughYesNoNoClamped faces you assume never slip
FrictionalYesIf force > μNNoBolted joints done properly (μ≈0.15–0.3 steel on steel), press fits, anything where slip matters
Why bonded can lieTwo plates bolted together and pulled apart: in reality the plates gap open away from the bolt and the bolt carries the load. With Bonded contact, the plates are welded along the entire touching face, so the joint is far stiffer and the stress flows through areas that would actually be separated. Fine for checking a part far from the joint; wrong for checking the joint itself.

Contact settings that matter

  • Contact vs Target. Each region has two sides. Rule of thumb: the contact side should be the finer mesh / convex / softer surface; the target the coarser / concave / stiffer one. For a pin in a hole: pin = contact, hole = target. Right-click → Flip Contact/Target.
  • Behavior: Symmetric (default for some types, both sides checked) vs Asymmetric (one-way). Asymmetric with the correct contact/target choice is cleaner for nonlinear contact.
  • Formulation: Program Controlled is fine to start. Augmented Lagrange (default for most nonlinear contact) and Pure Penalty allow a tiny penetration; Normal Lagrange allows none but is harder to converge; MPC is excellent for bonded (ties nodes with equations, no penetration, no fake stiffness).
  • Pinball Region: the search radius for detecting contact. If parts are slightly apart in CAD and Bonded isn't connecting them, the gap is bigger than the pinball. Increase it (or fix the CAD).
  • Interface Treatment (nonlinear types): Adjust to Touch closes small initial gaps so parts start touching. Very useful when CAD tolerance leaves a 0.01 mm gap that would otherwise let a part fly away.

Contact Tool: check before you solve

Right-click Connections → Insert › Contact Tool, then right-click it → Generate Initial Contact Results. You get a table of every contact region with its initial Status (Closed, Near, Far) and gap/penetration. Any nonlinear contact that's Far Open at the start isn't touching, and the parts may be unconstrained. Fix it before burning time on a solve.

After solving, insert a Contact Tool under Solution for Status, Pressure, Penetration, Sliding Distance. Contact pressure on a bolted joint face is very satisfying and very informative.

A joint connects two things (two bodies, or a body to ground) and specifies which of the 6 relative DOF are free. No contact detection, no mesh at the interface, just constraint equations. Insert from Connections › Joint.

  • Spring (Connections › Spring): linear stiffness (N/mm) and optional damping between two points or body-to-ground. Model a shock/coilover as a spring between its two mount points.
  • Beam connection (Connections › Beam): a circular line element between two faces with a real radius and material. A quick and decent way to represent a bolt without modeling a bolt body. You can read axial force and bending moment in it with a Beam Probe.

Rules of thumb

  • Through thickness: at least 2 quadratic elements (3+ is safer) through any wall that bends, if you care about bending stress there.
  • Around a fillet or hole where you read peak stress: aim for several elements around the arc (roughly 8–12 per 90° for good peaks). Then prove it with a convergence study (Module 13).
  • Use Named Selections on the critical faces so you can refine them repeatedly.
  • Coarse mesh far away is fine. Stiffness there barely affects your hot spot.

MODULE 09

Supports, every one of them

Supports are where your model meets the rest of the world. They're the single biggest source of wrong answers.

Supports live in the Environment context tab (click the Static Structural branch and the ribbon changes) under the Supports dropdown. Or right-click the Static Structural branch → Insert. Every support removes degrees of freedom: it sets displacements to known values (usually zero) and the solver calculates whatever reaction force is needed to hold them there.

The mental modelEvery support is a claim about the real world: "this face cannot move in this direction, no matter how much force it takes." Real attachments are never infinitely stiff, so every support is a little bit of a lie. The art is picking the lie that doesn't matter for your question.

Every support in detail

Fixed Support

Environment › Supports › Fixed Support · scope: face / edge / vertex / body

Every node on the selected geometry is held at zero displacement in X, Y and Z (and zero rotation on shells/beams). The most common support, and the most abused.

linearinfinitely stiffsingularity risk at edges
When to use, gotchas, real examples

Use when the face is welded, bolted, or clamped to something much stiffer than the part, and you care about stresses away from that face. Classic textbook cantilever.

Gotcha 1: it's too stiff. A real bolted base plate flexes; a fixed face doesn't. Poisson contraction is also blocked at the face, which creates fake stress concentrations right at the edge of the support. Don't report the peak stress at the edge of a Fixed Support. Look a short distance away, or make the support more realistic.

Gotcha 2: bolt holes. Putting Fixed Support on the inside faces of bolt holes is a common shortcut. It's stiffer than reality and gives high, mesh-dependent stresses at the hole edges. OK for global stiffness; not OK for checking the holes. Use Cylindrical Support or model the bolts.

Example: a Baja seat-mount tab welded to the frame: Fixed Support on the face welded to the tube, force on the bolt hole. You trust the stress at the tab's hole, and you'd check the weld separately.

Displacement

Environment › Supports › Displacement · scope: face / edge / vertex

Set each translational component (X, Y, Z) to Free, 0, or a value, in any coordinate system. A Fixed Support is just a Displacement with all three = 0.

linearenforced motionper-component
When to use, gotchas, real examples

Use when you need to constrain only some directions (a roller: Y = 0, X and Z free), or to push the part a known distance (enforced displacement: "how much force to deflect this spring tab 3 mm?" Then read the reaction force).

Coordinate System field lets you use a local or cylindrical system. Displacement in a cylindrical CS with Radial = 0 is another way to hold a hole round.

Gotcha: nonzero displacements on a face force every node on that face to move by exactly that amount, so the face stays perfectly flat and moves as a rigid plate. That's stiff, like Fixed.

Example: testing a leaf-spring or a flexure: Fixed one end, Displacement Y = −5 mm on the other end, read reaction to get stiffness k = F/δ.

Remote Displacement

Environment › Supports › Remote Displacement · scope: face / edge / vertex

Constrains a point in space (the remote point) that's tied to the selected face. You control all 6 DOF: UX, UY, UZ, ROTX, ROTY, ROTZ, each Free / 0 / value. This is how you give a solid face rotational constraints or freedoms.

linear*6 DOFrigid / deformable behavior
When to use, gotchas, real examples

Use when the support is something you didn't model: a hub, a bearing, a frame, the center of a pin. Put the remote point's Location where the real connection's center is, and free the rotations that the real connection allows.

Behavior matters a lot (see Module 11):

  • Rigid: the face is welded to an infinitely stiff spider. Face can't deform at all. Stiffens the part.
  • Deformable: the face can warp; the remote point gets the average motion. Usually more realistic. Check which one your version defaulted to; it's the first thing to look at in Details.
  • Coupled: all nodes share the same displacement in the constrained directions.

Example (pin joint): a clevis hole on a control arm pivot. Remote Displacement scoped to the hole face, location at hole center: UX=UY=UZ=0, ROTX=ROTY=0, ROTZ (pin axis) = Free. Now the arm can pivot but the pin holds it. Use a local coordinate system so Z is the pin axis.

Frictionless Support

Environment › Supports › Frictionless Support · scope: faces

Locks motion normal to the face (both into and out of it) and lets the face slide freely in-plane. Think "glued to a frictionless rail": can't lift off, can't push in, slides anywhere along the surface.

linearsymmetry planesrollers
When to use, gotchas, real examples

Use for symmetry. This is the symmetry boundary condition for solids: on a symmetry plane, material can't cross the plane but moves freely along it. Half a pressure vessel, half a beam, a quarter of a plate with a hole.

Use for rollers / slides where the part is held against a flat surface and can't lift (bolted but slotted holes, a part in a guide).

Gotcha: it holds the face in both directions. If the real part could lift off the floor, use Compression Only instead. And frictionless supports alone often leave the model free to slide; you usually need something else to stop in-plane rigid body motion.

Alternative: Mechanical also has a dedicated Symmetry object (right-click Model → Insert → Symmetry → Symmetry Region) which does the same thing and also mirrors the results for display.

Compression Only Support

Environment › Supports › Compression Only Support · scope: faces

Prevents the face from moving into the support, but lets it lift away. Internally it's frictionless contact against a rigid surface.

nonlinearlift-offbearing on a pin
When to use, gotchas, real examples

Use when a part rests on something and could rock or lift: a base plate on a floor, a lug pressed against a pin (only the loaded half of the hole carries load), a bracket bearing on a frame.

Classic use: pin-loaded hole. Compression Only on the inside of a hole correctly lets the unloaded side of the hole pull away, giving a realistic contact pressure distribution on the loaded side.

Gotchas: it's nonlinear, so solve time goes up. Since it's frictionless, nothing stops sliding along the face; add something else to stop in-plane motion, or the solver complains about rigid body motion. Check the reaction afterwards: if it's zero, the face lifted off entirely.

Cylindrical Support

Environment › Supports › Cylindrical Support · scope: cylindrical faces only

For holes and shafts. Three switches, each Fixed or Free: Radial (hole can't grow/shrink or shift), Axial (can't slide along axis), Tangential (can't spin about axis).

linearpins & boltssmall-deflection only
When to use, gotchas, real examples

Pivot / bushing: Radial Fixed, Axial Fixed, Tangential Free. Part can rotate about the pin but not translate.

Bolt hole (simplified): all three Fixed. Less stiff than a Fixed Support on the hole (which also stops the hole from ovalizing at all in every direction) but still holds the full circumference, including the side that would pull away in reality.

Gotcha: holding the entire 360° radially is stiffer than reality for a pin-loaded hole (in reality only the loaded side bears). Compression Only Support or real pin + frictionless contact is more accurate for hole stress. Cylindrical Support is fine for "everything else" stress.

Gotcha 2: the Tangential free option is only valid for small rotations. For large rotation use a Revolute joint.

Simply Supported

Environment › Supports › Simply Supported · scope: edges/vertices of surface or line bodies

Translations fixed, rotations free. The textbook "pinned" end of a beam or edge of a plate. Only meaningful on shell and beam models (solids have no rotations to free).

linearshell / beam only
When to use, gotchas, real examples

Use for a beam model of a simply-supported member, a plate whose edges sit on a frame but can rotate. On a frame modeled with line bodies, a Simply Supported vertex is a pin joint to ground.

On a solid? Use Displacement (or Remote Displacement with rotations free) on an edge or a thin strip. A Displacement constraint on a single edge of a solid works as a pin, but it creates a stress singularity along that edge.

Fixed Rotation

Environment › Supports › Fixed Rotation · scope: surface/line body geometry

The reverse of Simply Supported: rotations fixed, translations free. Each rotation component can be set Fixed or Free. Shell/beam models only.

linearshell / beam only
When to use, gotchas, real examples

Use for a guided end: an edge that can slide but must stay square (e.g. symmetry on a shell model, where the edge on the symmetry line must not rotate out of plane). Combine with a Displacement for the out-of-plane translation on a shell symmetry edge.

Elastic Support

Environment › Supports › Elastic Support · scope: faces

A bed of springs normal to the face. You enter Foundation Stiffness (pressure per displacement, e.g. N/mm³). Pressure on the face = stiffness × local normal displacement.

linearrubber mounts, soil
When to use, gotchas, real examples

Use for a part on rubber isolators, a footing on soil, a pad on foam. It's also a softer, more honest alternative to a Fixed Support when you know roughly how stiff the mating structure is.

Getting a value: if a mount has stiffness k (N/mm) and contact area A (mm²), foundation stiffness ≈ k/A (N/mm³).

Gotcha: it acts normal only (and in tension too). It doesn't stop in-plane sliding, so add something else for that.

Velocity (and other transient-only supports)

In Transient Structural you'll also see Velocity (enforced velocity) and in Explicit Dynamics a few more. Same idea as Displacement, just prescribing velocity. Not available in Static Structural.

Am I fully constrained? Two checks

  1. Turn Weak Springs Off in Analysis Settings and solve. If it fails with a rigid body / pivot warning, you're underconstrained.
  2. Or run a quick Modal analysis with your supports. Any mode at ~0 Hz is a rigid body motion you forgot to stop, and the mode shape shows you which way it's free.

Over-constraining is also a problem

Adding more supports than reality has (fixing both ends of a part that's really fixed at one end and slotted at the other) doesn't just change stress. It can completely change the load path. A thermal expansion problem with both ends fixed makes enormous fake stress, because the part can't grow. Ask of every support: does the real part push on something here, in this direction?

MODULE 10

Loads, every one of them

Loads build {F}. Same mental model as supports: every load is a claim about how force gets into the part.

Loads live in the Environment tab under Loads (structural loads), Inertial (gravity, acceleration, rotation), and Conditions (temperatures, joint loads). Most structural loads let you define direction in two ways:

  • Vector: pick a face/edge to define direction and type a magnitude. Arrow direction can be flipped in the graphics window.
  • Components: type X, Y, Z values in a chosen coordinate system. More precise, easier to check, works well with a local CS for angled loads.

And magnitude can be Constant, Tabular (varies per step), or a Function of time or position (e.g. linear pressure over a face).

Every load in detail

Force

Environment › Loads › Force · scope: face / edge / vertex

You type the total force. Mechanical divides it over the selected geometry as a uniform traction (force per area). Select three faces and type 300 N and you get 300 N total, split in proportion to area, not 300 N on each.

lineartotal, not per-face
When to use, gotchas, real examples

Use when load comes in through a face and you don't know the exact distribution: a load pad, a mounting face, a handle grip.

Gotcha 1: vertex and edge forces are singularities. A point force on a vertex puts finite force on zero area, so the local stress is theoretically infinite and will keep rising as you refine. Fine for global behavior; never read stress right at it. Use a small face (split a face in CAD to make a load patch) instead.

Gotcha 2: forces on holes. Force on the full inside of a hole pushes on the whole 360°, pulling on the back half. That's not what a pin does. Use Bearing Load, or model the pin.

Gotcha 3: Force doesn't add stiffness. The face is free to deform however it wants. Compare with Remote Force Rigid.

Pressure

Environment › Loads › Pressure · scope: faces

Force per area. Default Normal To: acts perpendicular to the face everywhere, even on curved faces, and a positive value pushes into the face. Can also be defined by vector or components (then it's a directional traction).

linearMPa = N/mm²follows curvature
When to use, gotchas, real examples

Use for internal pressure in a tank, cylinder, or brake caliper bore; air/water pressure on a panel; a known contact pressure.

Sign: positive pushes into the selected face. For internal pressure in a pipe, select the inside faces and enter a positive value: it pushes into the material from the inside, i.e. outward from the axis. Negative pulls (suction).

Gotcha: internal pressure in a closed tank also pushes on the end caps. If you've cut the model open, add the axial force the missing caps would have caused (p × π r²), or the axial stress will be missing.

Unit check: 1 MPa = 1 N/mm² ≈ 145 psi. 1 bar = 0.1 MPa.

Remote Force

Environment › Loads › Remote Force · scope: face / edge / vertex

A force applied at a point in space (default: centroid of the selection, change via Location X/Y/Z or by picking a vertex) and transferred onto the selected face. Because it's offset, the face receives the force and the moment F × r, automatically.

linearadds momentrigid / deformable
When to use, gotchas, real examples

Use when load is applied by something you didn't model, at a known point: the tire contact patch loading an upright through the spindle, a shock force at the shock eye center, a rider pushing on a pedal. This is the single most useful load for suspension and chassis parts.

Example: Baja upright. Model just the upright. Remote Force scoped to the spindle bore faces, Location at the tire contact patch (track and scrub radius offsets), components from your load case (e.g. 3g bump vertical + 1g cornering lateral). The upright sees the correct moments without modeling the hub, spindle, or wheel.

Behavior: Deformable (default) lets the face warp naturally. Rigid keeps the face's shape, which over-stiffens it and can raise stresses around the face edge. Module 11 has the details.

Check: after solving, a reaction probe on your supports should show the correct moment too, not just the force.

Moment

Environment › Loads › Moment · scope: face / edge / vertex

A pure moment (torque) applied to the selected geometry, about an axis you define by vector or components. Internally it's a remote load, so it also has a location and Rigid/Deformable behavior. Right-hand rule for direction.

linearN·mmright-hand rule
When to use, gotchas, real examples

Use for shaft torque (a CVT shaft, a half-shaft spline end), a bending moment from a member you cut off.

Torsion example: Fixed Support on one end of a shaft, Moment about the shaft axis on the other end face. Check against τ = T r / J.

Gotcha: units. Mechanical shows N·mm in the mm unit system; 100 N·m = 100,000 N·mm.

Bearing Load

Environment › Loads › Bearing Load · scope: one cylindrical face (or set of faces of one cylinder)

Simulates a pin or shaft pushing on a hole. The radial load is applied only on the compressive half of the hole, with a distribution that peaks in line with the load and falls to zero at 90°. Much closer to reality than a Force on the hole.

linearradial onlyholes, bushings
When to use, gotchas, real examples

Use for clevis holes, rod-end mounts, bearing bores, any pin-loaded lug where you don't want to model the pin and contact.

Gotchas: intended for radial load. If the pin also pushes axially, apply that as a separate Force. Scope only one cylinder per Bearing Load (two holes = two loads). Supports and the load can't share the exact same face.

Accuracy: it assumes a pin with zero clearance. With real clearance the contact arc is narrower and peak stress is higher. For a critical lug, model the pin with frictionless contact and compare.

Bolt Pretension

Environment › Loads › Bolt Pretension · scope: bolt shank cylindrical face, bolt body, or a beam connection

Mechanical slices the bolt with a plane and pulls the two halves together until the bolt carries the preload you specified. The clamped parts get squeezed, exactly like tightening a nut.

usually nonlinear (w/ contact)needs 2 steps
When to use, gotchas, real examples

Use when the bolted joint itself is what you're checking: does it slip, does it gap, what's the bolt stress?

Define By per step (in the Tabular Data table):

  • Load: apply this preload (N).
  • Adjustment: shorten the bolt by this much (mm).
  • Lock: freeze the bolt length where it ended up. External loads then add to bolt stress realistically.
  • Open: no pretension.

Standard setup: Analysis Settings → 2 steps. Bolt Pretension step 1 = Load, step 2 = Lock. External loads 0 in step 1, full in step 2. Frictional contact under the head/nut and between the plates; bonded (or shared topology) between nut and bolt if modeled.

Picking preload: F ≈ T / (K d), with T torque, d nominal diameter, K ≈ 0.2 (dry steel). M8 at 25 N·m → 25,000 / (0.2 × 8) ≈ 15,600 N.

Results: Bolt Tool under Solution → Adjustment and Working Load, so you can see the bolt force in each step.

Line Pressure

Environment › Loads › Line Pressure · scope: edges

Force per unit length (N/mm) along an edge. Defined by vector, components, or tangential.

linearN/mmedge singularity on solids
When to use, gotchas, real examples

Use for loads on shell edges or line bodies (distributed load on a beam model of a frame member), or a knife-edge load.

Gotcha: on a solid, an edge has zero area, so stress right along the edge is singular. Fine on shells and beams.

Hydrostatic Pressure

Environment › Loads › Hydrostatic Pressure · scope: faces

Pressure that increases linearly with depth: p = ρ g h. You give fluid density, the gravity/acceleration direction, and the location of the free surface.

lineartanks, hulls, ROVs
When to use, gotchas, real examples

Use for water tanks, dams, and underwater housings: e.g. an ROV or profiling float pressure hull at depth. For a submerged housing deep underwater, depth variation across the part is tiny compared with total depth, so plain uniform Pressure (ρ g h at the operating depth) is basically the same thing.

Gotcha: the free surface location is in the coordinate system you pick; get it wrong and pressure is negative on half the part.

Acceleration & Standard Earth Gravity

Environment › Inertial › Acceleration / Standard Earth Gravity · scope: whole model

Body loads: every bit of mass feels a force, so you need density. Standard Earth Gravity is 9806.6 mm/s² in the direction gravity pulls (pick −Y, etc). Acceleration is a general acceleration with a sign convention that trips everyone up.

linearneeds densitywatch the sign
When to use, gotchas, real examples

The sign convention: Acceleration means the supports are accelerating the part in that direction, so the part's inertia resists in the opposite direction. To mimic gravity pulling down (−Y), you apply Acceleration = +9806.6 mm/s² in +Y. Standard Earth Gravity skips this confusion: arrow points the way gravity pulls.

Use for self-weight of big parts, and g-load cases: a battery box or engine mount under a 5g vertical bump, a component under 1.5g braking. Fix the mounting points and apply the acceleration of the vehicle; the part's own mass loads itself.

Gotcha: point masses. If a heavy component isn't modeled (an engine on a mount), add it as a Point Mass (right-click Geometry → Insert → Point Mass) at its CG, scoped to the mount faces, so its inertia is included.

Rotational Velocity

Environment › Inertial › Rotational Velocity · scope: whole model or bodies

Spins the part about an axis you define. Each mass element feels centrifugal force m ω² r outward. Needs density.

linearrad/s or RPM
When to use, gotchas, real examples

Use for CVT sheaves, brake rotors, flywheels, fans, anything spinning fast. Check hoop stress in a thin ring: σ ≈ ρ ω² r².

Gotcha: it's not a torque. It only creates centrifugal load. Torque transmission is a separate Moment. There's also Rotational Acceleration (spin-up) which creates tangential inertial load.

Thermal Condition

Environment › Conditions › Thermal Condition · scope: bodies

Sets a uniform temperature on bodies. Compared with the environment's reference temperature (22 °C by default, set on the Static Structural branch), each body grows by α ΔT. Free expansion makes no stress; restrained expansion makes a lot.

linearneeds CTE
When to use, gotchas, real examples

MODULE 11

Remote points, Rigid vs Deformable, and Saint-Venant

How a load gets from "a point" onto "a face" decides your local stresses. Here's how to control it.

Remote Force, Remote Displacement, Moment, Point Mass, Springs and Joints all use a remote point: a single node off in space (or at a location you choose) tied to the selected face by constraint equations. You can create one explicitly (right-click Model → Insert → Remote Point) and reuse it: scope a Remote Force and a joint to the same point, for instance.

The Behavior setting

BehaviorFace can deform?Use when
RigidNoThe real mating part is much stiffer than this face: a steel spindle pressed into a bore, a thick hub flange. Also when you need moments transferred into a small face robustly.
DeformableYesThe mating part is similar stiffness or flexible, or you don't know. Default for most remote loads.
CoupledPartiallyAll nodes get the same displacement in the coupled DOF. Niche; for things like a face that must stay a constant distance in one direction.

Pinball region on remote points

A remote point only connects to nodes on the scoped face within its Pinball Region (All by default). Limit it if you only want part of a face to participate.

Saint-Venant's principle: why this all mostly works

The way a load is applied changes stress locally, but a few "characteristic dimensions" away (roughly the size of the loaded area or the part's thickness) the stress only depends on the resultant force and moment. So:

  • Fixed vs Cylindrical vs modeled bolt only matters near the hole.
  • Rigid vs Deformable only matters near the scoped face.
  • If your critical stress is far from all supports and loads, your simplifications are probably OK.
  • If your critical stress is at a support or load face, you must model that interface realistically (contact, real pin, bolt).
The habit to buildBefore trusting a peak stress, ask: "is this peak sitting right on a support or load?" If yes, it's probably an artifact of how you applied the BC. Move the BC further away (add more of the mating structure), or model the interface properly, and see if the peak survives.

Scoping tips that save hours

  • Split faces in CAD to make load patches (SpaceClaim: Design › Split, or sketch on a face and Pull with "imprint"). A 20 mm wide patch is much more realistic than an edge or vertex.
  • Named Selections by worksheet: right-click Named Selections → Insert → Named Selection, set Scoping Method to Worksheet to grab e.g. "all cylindrical faces with radius = 4 mm" (every M8 bolt hole) in one rule.
  • Show the load: click a load and the graphics show its arrows and scoped faces. Always look once before solving.

MODULE 13

Can I trust it? Singularities, convergence, verification

This module is the difference between "I ran FEA" and "I know the part is safe."

Stress singularities: the infinite-stress trap

Some spots have theoretically infinite stress in linear elasticity. The FEA value there isn't a real number; it's whatever your mesh size happens to make it. Refine the mesh and it keeps climbing forever. Common causes:

  • Sharp re-entrant corners (inside corner with zero fillet radius). Real parts always have some radius.
  • Point and edge loads/supports on solids (finite force, zero area).
  • Edges of Fixed Supports (the sudden switch from "can't move" to "free").
  • Edges of bonded contact between different-stiffness bodies.

A stress concentration is different: a hole or a real fillet has a high but finite peak (Kt × nominal). Refine the mesh and it converges to a value. That value is real and you must design for it.

What to do about a singularity

  • If it's not where you care (the corner of a fixed support), ignore it: scope your stress result away from it, or read stress a short distance away (Saint-Venant).
  • If it's where you care (an inside corner at the critical section), add the real fillet radius to the geometry. Then it becomes a finite, convergeable concentration.
  • If it's at a load/support, make the BC realistic (patch instead of point, model the pin).

Mesh convergence study (do this every time it matters)

  1. Make a Named Selection of the critical face (e.g. the fillet).
  2. Add a face Sizing on it. Solve with a coarse size. Record the peak stress scoped to that face.
  3. Halve the size. Solve. Record. Repeat 3–4 times.
  4. Plot peak stress vs element count (or 1/size). When the last two runs differ by less than ~5% (your call based on stakes), it's converged.
  5. If it never settles, you're at a singularity. See above.

Automatic way: right-click a stress result → Insert → Convergence. Set Allowable Change (e.g. 5%), and in Solution Details set Max Refinement Loops to 3–5. Mechanical will refine the mesh where needed and re-solve until the result stops changing. Scope the result first, or it will chase the singularity forever.

You can also turn on Parameter on the sizing and the peak stress and let Design Points run the study for you (Module 01).

Averaged vs unaveraged stress

Each element calculates its own stress at each node. Neighboring elements disagree at shared nodes; the default plot averages them, which looks smooth. Set Display Option to Unaveraged and you see the raw disagreement. Big jumps between neighbors = mesh too coarse there. Also try Structural Error (Solution → Insert → Stress Tool / Error): it highlights elements with the highest energy error, i.e. where to refine.

MODULE 14

Other analysis types: modal, buckling, thermal

Same Mechanical, different question. The supports chapter applies to all of them.

Modal (natural frequencies)

Drag Modal onto the schematic (or onto the Model cell of your static system to share geometry and mesh). No loads, just supports. Set Max Modes to Find in Analysis Settings (default 6). Solve, then the Tabular Data shows each mode's frequency; right-click → Create Mode Shape Results.

  • Mode shape magnitudes are meaningless. They're normalized. Only the shape and frequency matter.
  • Needs density. No density, no mass, no modes.
  • A mode at ~0 Hz means a rigid body motion: missing support or a disconnected part. Great debugging tool for static models too.
  • Why you care: if an excitation (engine firing frequency, tire imbalance, CVT) is near a natural frequency, you get resonance. Keep natural frequencies away from excitation (often a target like "first mode above X Hz").
  • Supports change frequencies a lot. A fixed vs bolted-on-rubber mount can halve the first mode.

Eigenvalue Buckling

Slender members in compression fail by buckling long before yield. Setup: a Static Structural with your supports and a load (often 1 N or the real load), then drag Eigenvalue Buckling onto the static system's Solution cell. Solve. The result is a Load Multiplier for each buckling mode:

Critical load = Load Multiplier × applied load (apply 1 N → multiplier = critical load in N)
Not conservativeLinear buckling assumes a perfect structure. Real ones have imperfections and residual stresses, so they buckle earlier, sometimes much earlier for thin shells. Apply a knockdown factor (or do a nonlinear buckling run with an imperfection seeded from the mode shape). Also check the mode: if the first mode is a local wrinkle of a thin wall instead of the global column bowing, that's the one that'll happen.

Steady-State Thermal

Solves for temperature, not displacement. Different "supports and loads":

Thermal stress (one-way coupled)

  1. Solve the Steady-State Thermal.
  2. Drag a Static Structural onto the Thermal's Solution cell (geometry/model are shared too).
  3. In Mechanical, the structural branch gets Imported Load → Imported Body Temperature. Right-click → Import Load.
  4. Add supports (carefully, don't over-constrain thermal growth) and any mechanical loads. Solve.

Brief: other systems you'll meet

  • Transient Structural: loads vary quickly enough that inertia matters (impacts, drops at low speed, shock events). Time-stepping, slower.
  • Harmonic Response: steady sinusoidal loading at a sweep of frequencies (vibration shaker test).
  • Random Vibration / Response Spectrum: PSD or shock spectrum inputs, linked from Modal.
  • Explicit Dynamics / LS-DYNA: crashes, high-speed impacts, large distortion.
  • Structural Optimization (Topology): drag onto a Static Structural's Solution cell; tells you where material isn't needed. Great for brackets and uprights. Always validate the redesigned part with a fresh static run.

Worked examples

Each one has a hand calc so you know what the right answer is before you click Solve. Build them yourself; they take 10–20 minutes each.

Example 1: Cantilever beam (your first model)

Geometry25 × 25 mm square bar, L = 300 mm
MaterialAl 6061-T6, E = 68.9 GPa, ρ = 2700 kg/m³
SupportFixed Support, one end face
LoadForce 500 N, −Y, other end face

Hand calc first

I = b⁴/12 = 25⁴/12 = 32,552 mm⁴ δ = F L³ / (3 E I) = 500 × 300³ / (3 × 68,900 × 32,552) = 2.01 mm M = F L = 500 × 300 = 150,000 N·mm (at the wall) σ = M c / I = 150,000 × 12.5 / 32,552 = 57.6 MPa (top/bottom at wall) σ at 25 mm from wall: M = 500 × 275 → σ = 52.8 MPa

Build it

  1. Toolbox → drag Static Structural onto the schematic.
  2. Engineering Data: add a 6061-T6 material (Module 02).
  3. Geometry → SpaceClaim: sketch a 25 mm square, Pull it 300 mm. Close SpaceClaim.
  4. Open Mechanical (double-click Model). Assign 6061 to the body.
  5. Mesh: body Sizing 5 mm (gives 5 quadratic elements through the depth). Generate.
  6. Click Static Structural. Fixed Support → one end face. Force → other end face, Components, Y = −500 N.
  7. Solution: insert Total Deformation, Directional Deformation (Y), Equivalent Stress, Normal Stress (X axis), Force Reaction probe on the Fixed Support.
  8. Solve.

What you should see

  • Directional deformation at the tip ≈ −2.0 mm. Within ~1% of hand calc (shear deflection adds a hair).
  • Force reaction Y = +500 N. Load path confirmed.
  • Normal stress X at the top surface 25 mm from the wall ≈ +52.8 MPa (tension on top), and −52.8 on bottom.
  • The max Equivalent Stress will likely show up at the corners of the fixed face, higher than 57.6, and it grows if you refine the mesh there. That's the Fixed Support singularity from Module 13. The physics answer is the beam-theory value away from the wall.

Try it: Change the material to Structural Steel without changing anything else. What happens to deflection and stress?

AnswerDeflection drops by the ratio of moduli, 68.9/200, to about 0.69 mm. Stress stays the same (≈57.6 MPa), because a statically determinate beam's stress depends only on geometry and load, not E. That's why a forgotten material assignment hides so well.

Extend it: Drag a Modal system onto this one's Model cell, keep the Fixed Support, and solve. What should the first frequency be?

Answerf₁ = (1.875²/2π) √(EI / (m L⁴)), m = ρA = 2.7e-9 t/mm³ × 625 mm² = 1.69e-6 t/mm. → f₁ ≈ 227 Hz. You'll get two modes very close to that (bending in Y and in Z, since the section is square), then a second bending pair around 6.27× higher, plus a torsion and axial mode.

Example 2: Plate with a hole, quarter symmetry, convergence study

GeometryPlate 100 wide × 300 long × 5 thick (mm), Ø20 hole at center
MaterialStructural Steel
Load10 kN tension along length
GoalPeak stress at the hole

Hand calc (Peterson)

σ_nom (net section) = P / ((W − d) t) = 10,000 / (80 × 5) = 25 MPa d/W = 0.2 → Kt ≈ 3 − 3.14(0.2) + 3.667(0.2)² − 1.527(0.2)³ = 2.51 σ_peak ≈ 2.51 × 25 = 62.7 MPa (at the hole edge, 90° from load direction)

Build it with symmetry

  1. Model only one quarter: 50 wide × 150 long × 5 thick with a quarter-circle notch of R10 at the corner. (Or full plate, then Split Body twice.)
  2. Frictionless Support on the two cut faces (the symmetry planes). These stop normal motion and let in-plane sliding happen, exactly like the missing three quarters would.
  3. Something must stop motion through the thickness (Z). Add a Displacement Z = 0 on one vertex, or (cleaner) also halve the thickness and put a third Frictionless Support on that cut.
  4. Load on the far end face: Pressure = −20 MPa (negative = pulling). Check: gross area of quarter end face = 50 × 5 = 250 mm², 20 × 250 = 5,000 N. That's half of 10 kN, correct for a quarter model since the end face of the quarter is half the width.
  5. Insert a Normal Stress (X along the load), scoped to the hole face. Solve at a few mesh sizes on the hole face: 4 mm, 2 mm, 1 mm, 0.5 mm.

What you should see

Peak normal stress at the hole converges toward ~62–63 MPa as the hole mesh gets finer. Coarse meshes under-predict it noticeably. Reaction on each symmetry plane: the one perpendicular to the load carries 5,000 N; the other carries ~0. This is the textbook demonstration that real stress concentrations converge (unlike Example 1's fixed-corner singularity).

Example 3: Thin-wall pressure vessel

GeometryTube, mean radius 50 mm, wall t = 2 mm
LoadInternal pressure p = 2 MPa, closed ends
ModelA short ring slice with symmetry
Hoop σθ = p r / t = 2 × 50 / 2 = 50 MPa Axial σz = p r / (2t) = 25 MPa von Mises ≈ √(σθ² − σθσz + σz²) = √(2500 − 1250 + 625) ≈ 43 MPa
  1. Model a quarter of the tube cross-section, 20 mm long (a curved strip).
  2. Frictionless Support on both longitudinal cut faces (quarter symmetry) and on one end face.
  3. Pressure +2 MPa on the inside face (pushes outward into the wall).
  4. The end caps aren't modeled, so replace their effect: Pressure −25 MPa on the other end face (pulls axially, giving the 25 MPa axial stress).
  5. Results: Normal Stress in a cylindrical coordinate system (create one with Z along the tube axis): Y = hoop, Z = axial. Equivalent Stress for comparison.

Expect: hoop ≈ 49–52 MPa (thick-wall theory gives a little more on the inside surface than the outside), axial ≈ 25 MPa, von Mises ≈ 43–45 MPa. If you forget step 4, axial stress comes out ~0 and von Mises is off. Classic "forgot the end cap load" mistake.

Example 4: Suspension upright with Remote Forces (Baja-style)

GeometryUpright only (no hub, spindle, wheel)
SupportsRemote Displacements at upper ball joint, lower ball joint, tie rod
LoadRemote Force at the tire contact patch
GoalStress in the upright, reaction at each pickup

This is the classic way to analyze a part that sits in a mechanism without modeling the whole car.

  1. Coordinates: import the upright in vehicle coordinates if you can (X fore-aft, Y lateral, Z vertical), so the load components from your load case go in directly.
  2. Ball joints: Remote Displacement scoped to each ball-joint bore, Location at the ball center. UX = UY = UZ = 0, all rotations Free (a spherical joint). Deformable behavior.
  3. Tie rod: Remote Displacement on the steering arm hole at the rod-end center. Only the translation along the tie rod axis is fixed (use a local CS aligned with the tie rod). Everything else Free. That's a two-force member: it can only push or pull along its axis.
  4. Load: Remote Force scoped to the spindle/bearing bore faces, Location at the tire contact patch. Components from your load case, e.g. vertical bump force and lateral cornering force.
  5. Check constraint: the two ball joints alone leave one motion free, spinning about the line through both ball centers (the steering axis). The tie rod stops it, so all 6 rigid body modes are covered. Note there's one redundant constraint: both ball joints resist force along the steering axis, so how that component splits between them depends on stiffness. In reality the control arms decide it; for the hand check below, compare the other components or assume a split.
  6. Results: Equivalent Stress scoped to the upright (excluding the ball-joint bore faces, where the BC artifacts live), Force Reaction probes on each Remote Displacement.
The check that makes this credibleDo a free-body diagram by hand: sum of forces and moments about the lower ball joint. The three reaction probes from Ansys should match your hand-calculated pickup loads. If they do, your load application and constraints are right, and the stress plot is worth looking at. Those same reaction forces are the loads for your control arm and tie rod analyses.

Refinements: if the bore stresses matter, model the bearing outer races or a dummy spindle with contact. If the steering arm is long and thin, check its local bending separately.

Example 6: Column buckling

GeometryØ10 mm solid steel rod, L = 500 mm
